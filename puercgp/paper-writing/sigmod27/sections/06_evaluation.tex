\subsubsection{Baselines.} We compare \system against five graph processing systems: Glign, ForkGraph, Gunrock, Groute, and iBFS. Glign is a state-of-the-art concurrent graph processing system for shared-memory architectures. ForkGraph is designed to efficiently execute graph algorithms involving multi-vertex traversals on CPUs. This design can also be viewed as an optimization for concurrent graph processing workloads. Gunrock and Groute are state-of-the-art GPU-based systems for single-query graph processing. We extend both systems with sequential variants (Gunrock-S and Groute-S) and concurrent variants (Gunrock-C and Groute-C). The sequential variants process multiple graph queries one at a time, whereas the concurrent variants use multiple GPU streams to process queries concurrently on a single shared graph copy. Finally, iBFS is a GPU-based system specifically optimized for concurrent BFS queries.

\subsubsection{Implementation and Hardware Settings}
\system is implemented as a header-only C++17/CUDA framework with approximately 8.5K lines of code. We compile it using GCC 12.2 and CUDA 12.8 on Debian 12, with -O3 optimization enabled. All experiments are conducted on the same CPU–GPU server. The host is equipped with two 2.40 GHz Intel Xeon Platinum 8260 CPUs, providing 48 physical cores and 96 hardware threads, along with 251 GiB of main memory. GPU workloads are executed on an NVIDIA Tesla V100-SXM2 GPU with 5,120 CUDA cores and 32 GiB of HBM2 memory.

\paragraph{Absolute performance.}
In Table~\ref{tab:n256-runtime}, bold values highlight the best result and
underlined values denote the second-best result in each row.
Ours is the fastest system for all 18 combinations of algorithm and dataset at
$N=256$.
Relative to the fastest baseline in each pair, it achieves a geometric-mean
speedup of $4.39\times$, with per-pair improvements ranging from
$1.66\times$ to $16.85\times$. Broken down by algorithm, the geometric-mean
speedups are $2.09\times$ for BFS, $7.27\times$ for SSSP, and $5.54\times$
for SSWP. The larger advantage on the two weighted traversals is consistent
with Ours sharing topology accesses while retaining typed per-query updates,
rather than launching or scheduling each traversal independently.

\paragraph{Scaling with the query window.}
The nearly parallel curves in Figure~\ref{fig:runtime-scaling-all} show that
the GPU systems generally preserve throughput as $N$ grows: from $N=32$ to
$N=256$, Ours changes geometric-mean throughput by $1.02\times$. Multi-stream
execution substantially improves Groute at $N=256$---by $2.40\times$ for BFS,
$1.80\times$ for SSSP, and $1.89\times$ for SSWP on geometric mean---but its
weighted traversals still remain behind Ours. Gunrock-C improves over
Gunrock-S by $1.28\times$ for BFS, while their weighted performance is nearly
identical ($1.01\times$). This contrast indicates that kernel concurrency alone
does not reliably capture cross-query reuse. Krill's geometric-mean throughput
falls to $0.57\times$ of its $N=32$ level, whereas Ours remains close to
constant throughput across the eightfold increase in query count.

Overall, Figure~\ref{fig:runtime-scaling-all} and
Table~\ref{tab:n256-runtime} show that Ours combines the lowest absolute
runtime with stable query-window scaling across all three traversal semantics.

\subsection{Sharing-aware Scheduler}
\label{sec:scheduler-ablation}

We fix the workload at $N=256$ and the resident batch size at $Q=32$.
Figure~\ref{fig:scheduler-ablation} reports matched ratios; values above one
indicate that the added component is beneficial.  Runtime includes online
grouping and offset planning.  We obtain the sharing factor (ASF) from a
separate instrumented run as $E_{\mathrm{pair}}/E_{\mathrm{union}}$, where
$E_{\mathrm{pair}}$ is the query-separated adjacency work and
$E_{\mathrm{union}}$ counts each active adjacency list once.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/06_evaluation/scheduler_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Effect of sharing-aware batching and bounded start offsets at
  $N=256,Q=32$.  Each marker is one graph--algorithm pair; the dashed line
  denotes no change.}
  \Description{Four scatter plots compare end-to-end runtime speedup and
  adjacency sharing-factor gain for query batching and start offsets across
  six graphs and three algorithms.}
  \label{fig:scheduler-ablation}
\end{figure*}

\subsubsection{Query Batching.}
Sharing-aware batching improves runtime on 16 of 18 graph--algorithm pairs,
with a geometric-mean speedup of $1.04\times$
(Figure~\ref{fig:scheduler-ablation}(a)).  More importantly, the measured ASF
increases by $1.05\times$ on geometric mean and on 17 of 18 pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:scheduler-ablation}(b)).  Thus, the scheduler's inexpensive
unweighted-landmark proxy generally places queries with overlapping realized
frontiers in the same batch, and the resulting reuse offsets its online cost.

\subsubsection{Start-offset Alignment.}
We next add bounded offsets while retaining the same 256 queries and $Q=32$.
Offsets improve FIFO by $1.16\times$ on geometric mean (17/18 wins), and add a
$1.21\times$ benefit after sharing-aware batching (18/18 wins).  The
corresponding ASF gains are $1.24\times$ and $1.31\times$, respectively
(Figure~\ref{fig:scheduler-ablation}(c--d)).  This stronger sharing signal
explains why offsets remain useful after batching rather than duplicating its
effect.  The logical completion-step stretch remains bounded: the geometric
means of the per-run p50/p95 ratios are 1.05/1.11 for FIFO and 1.04/1.12 for
sharing-aware batching.

\subsection{Hybrid Computation Strategy}
\label{sec:hybrid-ablation}

We evaluate the complete C9 configuration at $N=256,Q=32$.  In GraphWeft,
Push-only is a frontier-driven scatter, whereas Pull-only performs a
topology-driven gather over the graph; Hybrid uses the production threshold
selected on disjoint calibration sources.  Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid-pull}
reports fixed-mode runtime over Hybrid runtime, so a value above one favors
Hybrid.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/06_evaluation/hybrid_pull_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Push/Pull and pull-kernel ablations.  Panels (a--b) use the full C9
  execution at $N=256,Q=32$; panel (c) isolates matched pull kernels while
  varying the number of query columns.}
  \Description{Three plots show fixed-mode relative runtime, integrated
  segment-reduce versus fused-pull runtime, and pull microkernel speedup for
  query-column counts 8, 16, 32, and 64.}
  \label{fig:hybrid-pull}
\end{figure*}

\subsubsection{Push vs. Pull Execution.}
Hybrid is $1.04\times$ faster than Push-only and $1.01\times$ faster than
Pull-only on geometric mean, but the averages conceal substantial variation:
Hybrid wins only 3/18 and 10/18 pairs, respectively.  In particular, Hybrid
is $1.14\times$ faster than Push-only for SSSP on geometric mean, largely due
to a $2.44\times$ CP outlier, while Push-only is consistently slightly faster
for SSWP.  Fixed Pull likewise ranges from $2.30\times$ slower to
$2.32\times$ faster than Hybrid.  These results show that the frozen selector
is competitive in aggregate but is not an oracle, and we do not tune it on
the formal workload.

\subsubsection{Comparison of Different Pull Kernels.}
We compare our query-lane-coalesced, GE-SpMM-like pull with the extracted
segment-reduce control.  When integrated into the complete traversal, the two
are effectively tied: segment-reduce/fused runtime is $0.995\times$ on
geometric mean and fused wins 5/18 pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid-pull}(b)).  Isolating the matched kernels exposes the
benefit that other traversal stages hide: fused pull is $1.83\times$ faster at
$Q=32$ and $2.77\times$ faster at $Q=64$ across the six graphs
(Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid-pull}(c)).

Figure~\ref{fig:pull-kernel-profile} explains the kernel-level gap.  At
$Q=32$, fused pull improves load efficiency by $7.35$--$7.86\times$ and reads
$1.33$--$2.55\times$ fewer DRAM bytes.  Here, load efficiency is the fraction
of each fetched 32-byte global-memory sector actually requested by the warp.
The improvement is consistent across all six graphs, indicating that
coalescing both makes transferred sectors more useful and reduces off-chip
traffic.

\subsection{Multi-hop Propagation}
\label{sec:propagation-ablation}

Figure~\ref{fig:propagation-ablation} separates in-place propagation from the
two offline graph transformations.  Every new graph variant produces exactly
the same 256-query fingerprints as Original.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/06_evaluation/propagation_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Matched propagation ablations at $N=256,Q=32$.  Runtime and
  iteration ratios are before/after, so values above one indicate improvement.}
  \Description{Four scatter plots show runtime and iteration changes from
  in-place pull and from reorder-only, shortcut-only, and full preprocessing
  across six graphs and three algorithms.}
  \label{fig:propagation-ablation}
\end{figure*}

\subsubsection{Effect of In-place Pull.}
On Original, in-place pull improves runtime by $1.03\times$ on geometric mean
and wins 12/18 pairs.  On the preprocessed graph it also gives $1.03\times$,
but wins all 18 pairs.  The aggregate iteration reductions are only
$1.01\times$ and $1.00\times$, respectively
(Figure~\ref{fig:propagation-ablation}(a,c)).  Hence, the measured benefit does
not primarily come from fewer globally reported rounds; it comes from making
new values visible within a round and reducing the cost of reaching the same
fixed point.  This distinction prevents us from treating an in-place round as
equivalent to a synchronous superstep.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study on Preprocessing Method.}
Reordering alone is not sufficient: it yields $0.89\times$ geometric-mean
speedup and wins only 4/18 pairs.  Shortcut-only reduces iterations by
$1.61\times$ on every pair and improves runtime by $1.22\times$ on geometric
mean.  Full preprocessing further raises runtime speedup to $1.30\times$ while
retaining a $1.62\times$ iteration reduction
(Figure~\ref{fig:propagation-ablation}(b,d)).  The effect is algorithm
dependent.  Full preprocessing accelerates every SSSP and SSWP case, by
$2.11\times$ and $1.84\times$ on geometric mean, but slows all six BFS cases
to $0.57\times$ despite reducing their round counts.  Thus, contracting the
dependency path is useful only when the saved rounds outweigh the changed
per-round graph work.
